## Abstract

The development of increasingly capable artificial intelligence systems raises a fundamental architectural question: how should intelligence systems evaluate, select, compose, and improve their own computational structures?

Existing AI systems employ numerous evaluation mechanisms, including reward functions, learned value models, ranking systems, benchmarks, confidence estimators, and domain-specific constraints. However, these mechanisms are often designed for particular tasks, models, or optimization objectives.

The Universal Control Planes and Scoring Tree/Forest/Folding (UCPSF) research series proposes a broader direction: treating **Scoring Intelligence itself as a structurally learnable, transferable, and recursively improvable form of intelligence**.

This paper synthesizes the preceding seven studies into a long-term research framework.

We examine the progression from primitive scoring functions to Scoring Trees, Scoring Forests, Common Concept Core (CCC) extraction, recursive structural distillation, Perspective-Aware Meta-Scoring, and governed Structural Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI).

We introduce **Open-Ended Scoring Intelligence** as a research objective in which evaluation structures may expand, specialize, recombine, and improve through evidence-driven learning without requiring a permanently fixed evaluator topology.

The framework distinguishes reusable cross-domain scoring principles from Perspective-Specific evaluation structures. It proposes an Aspen Grove-inspired Core±Delta model in which shared structural roots coexist with diverse specialized branches.

We further examine how Universal Control Planes may coordinate heterogeneous Localization Engines, Per-Node Intelligence Engines, and portable structural intelligence components across independent systems.

Particular attention is given to Collective Structural Learning, evaluation governance, counter-evidence, structural diversity, and the limits of recursive evaluator improvement.

Finally, we propose falsifiable hypotheses, experimental milestones, and criteria for distinguishing genuine structural intelligence growth from increasingly elaborate but ineffective system complexity.

The central conclusion is a research proposition rather than an established empirical result:

> Scoring Intelligence may become a reusable and recursively improvable architectural capability that guides the evolution of heterogeneous AI systems while remaining subject to independent evidence and explicit governance.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Evolution of AI Evaluation

Artificial intelligence systems require mechanisms for distinguishing desirable outcomes from undesirable ones.

Such mechanisms include:

- Loss functions;
- Reward models;
- Ranking systems;
- Confidence estimates;
- Utility functions;
- Constraint checkers;
- Learned evaluators;
- Human feedback;
- External outcome measurements.

These mechanisms have played important roles in machine learning, reinforcement learning, search, planning, and modern generative AI.

However, an increasingly complex intelligence system may need to evaluate more than individual outputs.

It may need to evaluate:

- Alternative reasoning paths;
- Context localization methods;
- Specialized intelligence engines;
- Calling Graphs;
- Multi-agent workflows;
- Structural representations;
- Candidate architecture modifications;
- Evaluators themselves.

This creates an opportunity to investigate evaluation as a structured intelligence capability.

### 1.2 From Fixed Evaluation to Structural Evaluation

A conventional evaluator may be represented as:

\[
S(x)
\]

where \(x\) is the object being evaluated.

A Perspective-aware evaluator may instead be represented as:

\[
S(x\mid P,E)
\]

where:

- \(P\): Perspective;
- \(E\): Available evidence.

A structured evaluator may contain multiple scoring nodes, branches, and dependencies.

A collection of such evaluators forms a Scoring Forest.

The evaluation architecture can then become an object of structural learning.

### 1.3 The Open-Ended Hypothesis

We propose:

> Evaluation architectures need not remain fixed. They may grow, specialize, recombine, and improve through validated structural learning.

The term *open-ended* does not imply unlimited improvement, guaranteed progress, or unrestricted autonomy.

It describes an architectural research direction in which the space of evaluation structures is not permanently closed.

### 1.4 Scope of This Paper

This paper synthesizes the UCPSF research series and develops future hypotheses.

It does not claim to have demonstrated artificial general intelligence, autonomous recursive improvement, or universal evaluation capability.

The objective is to establish a coherent research agenda with testable architectural propositions.

## 3. From Scoring Functions to Scoring Intelligence

### 3.1 Scoring Functions

A scoring function assigns an evaluation result to an object.

For example:

\[
S(x)=w^\top f(x)
\]

where:

- \(f(x)\): Feature representation;
- \(w\): Evaluation weights.

This formulation is useful when relevant dimensions and weights are sufficiently well specified.

### 3.2 Scoring Intelligence

Scoring Intelligence extends beyond applying a fixed function.

It may involve:

- Selecting evaluation dimensions;
- Identifying relevant metrics;
- Constructing evaluation structures;
- Choosing evaluators;
- Combining evidence;
- Managing uncertainty;
- Revising evaluation criteria;
- Detecting evaluator failures.

### 3.3 A Functional Distinction

We distinguish:

**Scoring Function**

A mechanism that computes an evaluation.

**Scoring Intelligence**

A capability that constructs, selects, applies, evaluates, and improves evaluation mechanisms.

### 3.4 Human Judgment as an Architectural Analogy

Human evaluation often involves multiple interacting considerations.

For example, an engineering decision may consider:

- Technical feasibility;
- Cost;
- Risk;
- Maintainability;
- Timing;
- Strategic advantage.

These considerations may change according to context.

The analogy suggests the usefulness of structured, Perspective-aware evaluation.

It does not establish that human judgment can be fully represented by a Scoring Tree.

### 3.5 Research Implication

The ability to select and improve evaluators may become as important as the ability to generate candidate solutions.

## 4. Three Scoring Operator Families

### 4.1 Operator-Level Structural Learning

UCPSF-002 introduces three candidate families of scoring-related operations.

These are useful research categories, not an exhaustive classification of evaluation methods.

### 4.2 Value-Based Folding

Value-Based Folding modifies numerical evaluation parameters while preserving an intended decision structure.

Potential targets include:

- Weights;
- Thresholds;
- Metric coefficients;
- Score normalization;
- Calibration parameters.

### 4.3 Branching-Based Folding

Branching-Based Folding attempts to reduce or reorganize evaluation branches.

Its objective may include:

- Removing redundancy;
- Sharing repeated substructures;
- Reducing evaluation cost;
- Improving structural reuse.

Branch reduction must be distinguished from arbitrary pruning.

A branch may be essential for rare but consequential cases.

### 4.4 ANN Scoring Nodes

Learned ANN scorers may approximate more expensive evaluation mechanisms.

Potential benefits include:

- Lower repeated evaluation cost;
- Fast ranking;
- Specialized scoring;
- Distillation of complex evaluation behavior.

Their limitations include:

- Distribution shift;
- Calibration error;
- Proxy exploitation;
- Hidden failure modes.

### 4.5 Operator Composition

The three families may be combined.

For example:

```text
Rich Scoring Tree
       |
       v
Branching-Based Folding
       |
       v
Value-Based Refinement
       |
       v
ANN Scorer Distillation
       |
       v
Independent Validation
```

The sequence is illustrative.

Different applications may require different orders or combinations.

## 5. Scoring Trees, Forests, and Structural Diversity

### 5.1 Scoring Trees

A Scoring Tree organizes evaluation into connected nodes.

A node may represent:

- A metric;
- A constraint;
- A learned scorer;
- A decision rule;
- A specialized evaluator;
- An aggregation operation.

### 5.2 Scoring Forests

A Scoring Forest contains multiple evaluation structures.

Conceptually:

\[
\mathcal{F}_S=\{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n\}
\]

Different trees may correspond to:

- Different domains;
- Different Perspectives;
- Different task classes;
- Different risk profiles;
- Different evaluation strategies.

### 5.3 Diversity as a Resource

Evaluation diversity may help expose disagreements and blind spots.

However, diversity alone does not guarantee better decisions.

A forest containing many correlated evaluators may provide less independent information than its size suggests.

### 5.4 Structural Diversity and Behavioral Diversity

Two evaluators may differ structurally but produce similar decisions.

Conversely, similar-looking evaluation structures may behave differently under distribution shift.

Therefore, structural comparison should be complemented by behavioral validation.

### 5.5 The Long-Tail Problem

Rare cases may depend on evaluation branches that appear unimportant under average-case testing.

A folding operation that removes such branches may reduce computational cost while increasing catastrophic or high-impact failure risk.

This motivates explicit preservation of counter-evidence and applicability boundaries.

## 6. The Aspen Grove Model of Structural Intelligence

### 6.1 The Aspen Metaphor

A grove of quaking aspens provides a useful analogy for the relationship between individual structures and shared foundations.

Individual trees have distinct trunks and branches.

Yet some aspen groves may share connected underground root systems.

The analogy motivates a model in which multiple Scoring Trees share reusable structural cores while retaining specialized differences.

### 6.2 Core Plus Delta

We propose:

\[
T_i \approx K \oplus \Delta_i
\]

where:

- \(T_i\): Individual Scoring Tree;
- \(K\): Shared Common Concept Core;
- \(\Delta_i\): Specialized structural or behavioral differences.

The operator \(\oplus\) denotes a conceptual composition, not necessarily an invertible algebraic operation.

### 6.3 Shared Roots

A reusable CCC may encode:

- Evaluation principles;
- Common decision dependencies;
- Constraint structures;
- Evidence requirements;
- Repeated scoring relationships.

### 6.4 Specialized Branches

Specialized Delta may preserve:

- Domain-specific metrics;
- Risk-sensitive checks;
- Local context;
- Alternative decision rules;
- Rare-case handling.

### 6.5 Why Not Fold Everything?

A universal scoring structure that removes all differences may become too generic to make useful decisions.

The objective is not maximal compression.

It is reusable structural commonality with sufficient preservation of meaningful differences.

### 6.6 A Forest of CCCs

A large Scoring Forest may contain multiple CCC families.

Some may be domain-specific.

Others may transfer across domains.

Still others may represent only temporary similarities.

The architecture should allow these structures to coexist without forcing premature unification.

### 6.7 Architectural Principle

> Preserve commonality where supported by evidence, and preserve diversity where it carries decision-relevant information.

## 7. Universal CCC and Perspective-Specific CCC

### 7.1 Two Complementary Objectives

The search for reusable evaluation structures creates a tension.

One objective is to discover broadly transferable principles.

Another is to preserve highly effective Perspective-specific evaluators.

Both are necessary research directions.

### 7.2 Universal CCC

A Universal CCC is a candidate structural core intended to apply across multiple domains or Perspectives.

Examples might include general patterns for:

- Constraint checking;
- Evidence aggregation;
- Uncertainty escalation;
- Cost-aware evaluation;
- Independent validation.

Universality must be demonstrated across specified domains rather than assumed from structural similarity.

### 7.3 Perspective-Specific CCC

A Perspective-Specific CCC captures evaluation patterns useful under particular conditions.

For example:

- Low-latency operation;
- Safety-critical decisions;
- Exploratory research;
- Competitive optimization;
- Domain-specific engineering.

### 7.4 A Layered CCC Organization

```text
Candidate Cross-Domain CCCs
             |
      +------+------+
      |             |
      v             v
Domain CCC A     Domain CCC B
      |             |
      v             v
Perspective CCC  Perspective CCC
      |             |
      v             v
Specialized Delta Specialized Delta
```

This is a candidate organizational pattern, not a requirement that all CCCs form a strict tree.

A graph or forest may be more appropriate.

### 7.5 Promotion and Demotion

A Perspective-specific CCC may gain broader applicability after successful cross-domain validation.

A previously broad CCC may require specialization when counter-evidence reveals hidden limitations.

Thus, structural learning should support both generalization and restriction.

### 7.6 Non-Monotonic Evolution

A CCC may be:

- Promoted;
- Revised;
- Split;
- Specialized;
- Merged;
- Retired.

The architecture must not assume that every new revision is superior.

## 8. Recursive CCC Distillation

### 8.1 From Samples to Candidate Cores

Repeated Scoring Tree observations may reveal recurring structural patterns.

The proposed distillation process begins with sampled evaluators.

### 8.2 Intra-Family Folding

Trees belonging to a related evaluation family may be compared to identify candidate commonality.

### 8.3 Unaligned AND Folding

Different trees may express related evaluation behavior through different representations or topologies.

Unaligned AND Folding investigates whether their commonality can be identified without requiring exact structural alignment.

### 8.4 Cross-Family Distillation

Candidate cores from different scoring families may be compared.

This may reveal higher-level evaluation principles.

### 8.5 Recursive Growth

A candidate recursive process is:

```text
Sampled Scoring Trees
          |
          v
Structural / Behavioral Comparison
          |
          v
Candidate CCC Extraction
          |
          v
Independent Validation
          |
          v
CCC Registry
          |
          v
New Samples and Counter-Evidence
          |
          v
Revision / Specialization / Growth
```

### 8.6 Distillation Is Not Proof

Structural similarity does not prove behavioral equivalence.

Successful behavior on sampled tasks does not prove universal applicability.

A candidate CCC must therefore preserve:

- Supporting evidence;
- Counter-evidence;
- Applicability boundaries;
- Version;
- Uncertainty;
- Validation status.

### 8.7 Stopping Conditions

Recursive distillation should stop or escalate when:

- Additional samples provide insufficient information;
- Validation fails;
- Cost exceeds expected benefit;
- Structural ambiguity remains unresolved;
- Required evidence is unavailable.

## 9. Open-Ended Scoring Intelligence

### 9.1 Definition

We propose the following working definition:

**Open-Ended Scoring Intelligence is the capability of an intelligence system to discover, construct, select, revise, and extend evaluation structures over time through evidence-driven structural learning, without restricting all future evaluators to a permanently fixed topology or predefined set of scoring dimensions.**

### 9.2 Open-Ended Does Not Mean Unbounded

The architecture may remain subject to:

- Computational budgets;
- Policy constraints;
- Validation requirements;
- Human authorization;
- Structural compatibility;
- Resource availability.

Open-ended structural search and bounded runtime governance are compatible.

### 9.3 Sources of Growth

Potential sources of new evaluation structures include:

1. New task domains;
2. New Perspectives;
3. New evidence;
4. Counter-evidence;
5. New intelligence engines;
6. New Localization Engines;
7. New scoring operators;
8. New structural representations;
9. New external tools;
10. New collaborative contributions.

### 9.4 Beyond Gap Bridging

Structural growth need not arise only from identifying a gap between current and desired states.

It may also arise from:

- New interface opportunities;
- New component combinations;
- Alternative evaluation perspectives;
- Reusable workflow discovery;
- Previously unavailable computational capabilities.

### 9.5 Growth Versus Accumulation

Adding more evaluators does not necessarily constitute intelligence growth.

A meaningful improvement should produce evidence of better:

- Decisions;
- Reliability;
- Transfer;
- Efficiency;
- Robustness;
- Structural reuse.

### 9.6 Growth Versus Complexity

A larger Scoring Forest may become more expensive and harder to govern.

The system should evaluate structural growth against its marginal benefit.

### 9.7 Research Hypothesis

Open-ended structural growth may be useful when new evaluation requirements cannot be adequately represented by existing scoring structures.

Whether such growth improves outcomes must be tested.

## 10. Meta-Scoring as a Growth Mechanism

### 10.1 Evaluating Evaluators

Meta-Scoring Intelligence evaluates the suitability and reliability of scoring mechanisms.

It may assess:

- Calibration;
- Decision quality;
- Robustness;
- Applicability;
- Cost;
- Transferability;
- Constraint preservation.

### 10.2 Evaluator Discovery

A meta-evaluator may identify situations where existing Scoring Trees fail.

Such failures may motivate:

- New scoring dimensions;
- Specialized branches;
- Alternative operator compositions;
- New learned evaluators.

### 10.3 Evaluator Evolution

Meta-Scoring may guide structural revisions.

However, evaluator revisions must be independently validated.

### 10.4 The Recursive Evaluation Problem

If evaluators can improve evaluators, a question arises:

Who evaluates the meta-evaluator?

The architecture should not rely on unlimited self-referential recursion.

### 10.5 Bounded Meta-Evaluation

Possible safeguards include:

- Fixed reference tests;
- Independent external validators;
- Human-defined constraints;
- Limited recursion depth;
- Versioned evaluator baselines;
- Separate promotion authority.

### 10.6 Two-Level Growth

We distinguish:

**Intelligence Growth**

Improving the systems that perform tasks.

**Evaluation Growth**

Improving the systems that judge task performance and structural changes.

The two processes may reinforce one another, but their validation should remain appropriately separated.

## 15. Structural RSI and the Limits of Self-Improvement

### 15.1 Recursive Improvement Opportunities

Structural RSI may modify:

- Intelligence Engines;
- Localization structures;
- Scoring mechanisms;
- Calling Graphs;
- Context representations;
- CCCs;
- Dispatch policies.

### 15.2 Evaluator Dependence

An RSI system can optimize only according to available evaluation signals.

Weak or misleading evaluators may direct improvement toward undesirable outcomes.

### 15.3 Rich Evaluators

Scoring Forests and Meta-Scoring may provide richer evaluation than a single reward signal.

However, richer evaluation does not automatically imply correct evaluation.

### 15.4 Independent Evidence

Structural changes should be tested against external outcomes and appropriately independent references.

### 15.5 Self-Modification Boundaries

The system should not automatically gain authority to alter its own governance constraints.

### 15.6 Improvement Is Not Guaranteed

Recursive modification may produce:

- Genuine improvement;
- No meaningful change;
- Increased cost;
- Reduced reliability;
- Evaluation drift;
- New failure modes.

### 15.7 Research Principle

> Recursive improvement should be judged by validated external consequences, not by the mere existence of recursive modification.

## 16. A Falsifiable Research Agenda

### 16.1 Why Falsifiability Matters

A broad architectural framework can become difficult to evaluate if every result is interpreted as supporting the theory.

UCPSF should therefore define measurable hypotheses and explicit failure conditions.

### 16.2 Hypothesis H1 — Structural Evaluation Transfer

**Claim:**

Validated Scoring CCCs can transfer useful evaluation behavior across related tasks.

**Test:**

Compare transferred CCC evaluators against task-specific evaluators and fixed baselines.

**Potential falsification:**

Transfer consistently fails to improve decision quality, cost, or reliability under controlled conditions.

### 16.3 Hypothesis H2 — Scoring Forest Advantage

**Claim:**

Selected Scoring Forest configurations can outperform single evaluators on sufficiently heterogeneous tasks.

**Test:**

Compare fixed single evaluators, ensembles, and structurally selected forests.

**Potential falsification:**

Scoring Forests provide no net benefit after accounting for computational cost and correlated errors.

### 16.4 Hypothesis H3 — Recursive CCC Distillation

**Claim:**

Recursive CCC distillation can discover reusable evaluation structures that retain meaningful decision behavior.

**Test:**

Evaluate compressed structures on held-out tasks, rare cases, and distribution shifts.

**Potential falsification:**

Distilled CCCs consistently lose essential decision information or fail to transfer.

### 16.5 Hypothesis H4 — Perspective-Aware Meta-Scoring

**Claim:**

Meta-Scoring can improve evaluator selection under changing Perspectives.

**Test:**

Compare fixed selection, simple routing, and Meta-Scoring under controlled Perspective changes.

**Potential falsification:**

Meta-Scoring does not improve downstream utility or cost-adjusted performance.

### 16.6 Hypothesis H5 — Dual-Hybrid Intelligence

**Claim:**

Adaptive selection among Localization and Per-Node Engines can improve outcomes on heterogeneous workloads.

**Test:**

Compare integrated models, fixed hybrid pipelines, and adaptive hybrid configurations.

**Potential falsification:**

Adaptive composition overhead consistently exceeds its benefits.

### 16.7 Hypothesis H6 — Governed Structural RSI

**Claim:**

Contract-first validation and independent promotion controls can reduce harmful or ineffective structural modifications.

**Test:**

Compare controlled and uncontrolled structural modification campaigns in isolated environments.

**Potential falsification:**

The governance mechanisms fail to improve promotion reliability or impose unacceptable overhead.

### 16.8 Hypothesis H7 — Collective Structural Learning

**Claim:**

Portable, validated structural components can improve learning efficiency across independent systems.

**Test:**

Measure cross-system reuse under controlled transfer conditions.

**Potential falsification:**

Imported structures consistently require more adaptation than independent local development.

## 18. What Would Count as Genuine Progress?

### 18.1 Beyond Repository Growth

A larger collection of Scoring Trees or CCCs is not necessarily more intelligent.

### 18.2 Beyond Architectural Complexity

A larger Hybrid Architecture is not necessarily better.

### 18.3 Beyond Internal Scores

Higher internal scores do not necessarily indicate better external outcomes.

### 18.4 Proposed Progress Criteria

Meaningful progress should be supported by evidence of at least one of the following, without unacceptable regression elsewhere:

1. Improved task outcomes;
2. Better cost-adjusted decisions;
3. Greater robustness;
4. Better calibration;
5. Improved transfer;
6. Reduced repeated computation;
7. More reliable structural promotion;
8. Better constraint preservation;
9. More effective reuse;
10. Improved adaptability to new conditions.

### 18.5 Net Structural Value

A conceptual measure is:

\[
V_{\mathrm{structural}}
=
B_{\mathrm{validated}}
-
C_{\mathrm{lifecycle}}
-
R_{\mathrm{residual}}
\]

where:

- \(B_{\mathrm{validated}}\): Validated benefits;
- \(C_{\mathrm{lifecycle}}\): Development, execution, validation, and maintenance costs;
- \(R_{\mathrm{residual}}\): Risk-adjusted residual losses.

The terms must be expressed in compatible units before numerical aggregation.

Alternatively, they may be handled through multi-objective evaluation.

### 18.6 The Simplicity Test

A proposed structural mechanism should be compared against simpler alternatives.

If a fixed model or lightweight workflow performs equally well at lower cost, the more complex architecture may not be justified.

## 19. Risks, Boundaries, and Unresolved Problems

### 19.1 Universal Evaluation May Be Impossible

Some objectives may be incomparable or context-dependent.

A universal evaluation architecture need not imply a universal scalar objective.

### 19.2 Perspective Conflict

Different stakeholders may have incompatible goals or constraints.

The Control Plane must not assume these conflicts can always be resolved through numerical optimization.

### 19.3 Evaluator Capture

A system may manipulate its own evaluation process.

Independent validation and governance boundaries are necessary but may not eliminate all risks.

### 19.4 Structural Overcompression

Folding may erase rare but important distinctions.

### 19.5 Structural Overgrowth

Open-ended growth may produce excessive complexity.

### 19.6 Correlated Evidence

Multiple evaluators may share hidden dependencies or biases.

### 19.7 Portability Failure

A CCC useful in one environment may fail in another.

### 19.8 Distributed Governance

Federated systems may encounter conflicts over authority, trust, and evidence.

### 19.9 Recursive Instability

Repeated structural modification may introduce regressions and unexpected interactions.

### 19.10 Validation Limits

Finite tests cannot establish universal correctness.

### 19.11 Open Problem

The largest unresolved challenge may not be generating more intelligence components.

It may be developing sufficiently reliable mechanisms for evaluating and governing their interactions and evolution.

## 22. Conclusion

The UCPSF research series began with a question about the limitations of primitive scoring mechanisms in complex AI Relay and Structural RSI systems.

It developed that question into a broader architectural framework.

The framework proposes that evaluation should be treated as a structured and potentially learnable intelligence capability.

Scoring Trees and Forests organize heterogeneous evaluators.

Unaligned AND Folding and recursive CCC distillation investigate reusable evaluation structures.

Perspective-Aware Meta-Scoring supports evaluator selection and revision.

Dual-Hybrid Intelligence expands the space of computational architectures.

Universal Control Planes coordinate execution, evaluation, evidence, policy, and structural evolution.

This final paper extends those components toward Open-Ended Scoring Intelligence and Collective Structural Learning.

Five conclusions summarize the proposed research direction.

**First**, scoring mechanisms can be studied as structural intelligence objects rather than only fixed mathematical functions.

**Second**, reusable CCCs and Specialized Delta may provide a useful balance between generalization and diversity.

**Third**, Meta-Scoring and independent validation are essential research components for recursively evolving evaluation architectures.

**Fourth**, Hybrid Localization and Per-Node Intelligence create an architectural design space in which computational structures may be selected and revised according to task requirements.

**Fifth**, open-ended structural discovery must be distinguished from unrestricted deployment authority.

The central thesis of the UCPSF series is:

> **Scoring Intelligence itself can become a structurally learnable, transferable, and recursively improvable form of intelligence.**

The corresponding architectural proposition is:

> **Future AI systems may increasingly depend on governed mechanisms that select, evaluate, compose, and evolve heterogeneous intelligence structures, rather than relying exclusively on fixed model architectures and fixed evaluation functions.**

These propositions are not yet established empirical conclusions.

They define a research program whose value must be demonstrated through formalization, controlled experimentation, independent validation, and longitudinal evidence.

The objective is not simply to create more complex AI systems.

It is to develop intelligence systems whose structural organization, evaluation mechanisms, and evolutionary processes can become more effective, reusable, and governable over time.
