## Abstract

Complex AI Relay and Structural Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) systems increasingly employ heterogeneous scoring mechanisms to evaluate candidate solutions, select computational paths, allocate resources, and govern structural changes.

These scoring mechanisms may differ in topology, representation, operator semantics, parameters, and application domains. Nevertheless, they may contain reusable evaluation and control principles.

UCPSF-003 introduced Scoring Trees, Scoring Forests, and Unaligned AND Folding as a framework for discovering such principles without requiring predefined structural alignment.

This paper extends that framework into a progressive algorithm for **Common Concept Core (CCC) Extraction and Recursive Distillation**.

The proposed approach treats each Scoring Tree as a source of structural and behavioral evidence. Multiple trees are localized, compared, aligned, and folded into candidate CCCs. These cores are then tested, specialized, revised, and recursively compared with additional evidence.

The resulting system is not necessarily a single universal scoring tree. Instead, it may form a hierarchical and partially overlapping forest of shared cores, specialized deltas, applicability boundaries, and validation records.

We introduce a six-stage algorithm covering primitive scoring modeling, sampled forest construction, intra-family Unaligned AND Folding, recursive CCC distillation, cross-family core extraction, and evidence-driven structural growth.

We further propose explicit mechanisms for CCC promotion, specialization, splitting, rollback, and retirement.

A central principle is that **recursive distillation must preserve decision-relevant distinctions and remain open to counter-evidence**. Increasing sample volume or structural compression alone does not establish improvement.

The framework provides a candidate pathway for converting heterogeneous evaluation mechanisms into reusable, transferable, and recursively improvable Scoring Intelligence.

**Keywords:** Common Concept Core, Recursive Distillation, Scoring Forest, Unaligned AND Folding, Structural Intelligence, Structural Folding, Core and Delta, Counter-Evidence, Universal Control Plane, Structural RSI, AI Relay, Evidence-Driven Growth

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 From Structural Commonality to Structural Growth

UCPSF-001 established Generalized Scoring Intelligence as a research direction.

UCPSF-002 introduced three candidate operator families:

1. Value-Based Folding;
2. Branching-Based Folding;
3. ANN Scoring Nodes.

UCPSF-003 developed Scoring Trees, Scoring Forests, and Unaligned AND Folding.

Together, these papers suggest that scoring mechanisms can be represented as structural intelligence objects rather than isolated evaluation functions.

However, identifying commonality among a small collection of Scoring Trees is only the beginning.

A more important question follows:

**Can repeated structural comparison and evidence-driven Folding produce progressively more reusable and reliable evaluation intelligence?**

This paper investigates that question.

### 1.2 The Recursive Distillation Hypothesis

Suppose an intelligence system accumulates many scoring structures through:

- AI Relay;
- Automated engineering;
- Multi-agent evaluation;
- Structural search;
- Recursive self-improvement;
- Collective learning.

These structures may encode recurring principles.

Rather than independently rediscovering similar evaluation logic, the system may attempt to extract and reuse common cores.

New samples can then challenge, refine, or specialize existing cores.

We propose:

> Scoring intelligence may evolve through repeated extraction, validation, and revision of reusable Common Concept Cores across heterogeneous evaluation structures.

This is a research hypothesis, not a demonstrated universal learning law.

### 1.3 Recursive Does Not Mean Automatically Improving

A recursive process may amplify errors as well as useful knowledge.

Repeated Folding may:

- Remove important exceptions;
- Reinforce correlated biases;
- Overgeneralize limited evidence;
- Produce evaluator drift;
- Create increasingly complex but ineffective abstractions.

Accordingly, the proposed algorithm requires explicit validation, counter-evidence, applicability boundaries, and reversible promotion decisions.

## 2. Formal Objects and Terminology

### 2.1 Scoring Tree

A Scoring Tree is represented as:

$$
T_i = (V_i,E_i,O_i,\Sigma_i,P_i,C_i)
$$

where:

- $V_i$: Scoring nodes;
- $E_i$: Dependency relationships;
- $O_i$: Scoring and decision operators;
- $\Sigma_i$: Evaluation semantics;
- $P_i$: Perspective metadata;
- $C_i$: Contracts and constraints.

### 2.2 Scoring Forest

A Scoring Forest is a collection of Scoring Trees:

$$
\mathcal{F} = \{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_n\}
$$

The trees may differ in domain, perspective, granularity, or implementation.

### 2.3 Common Concept Core

A candidate Scoring CCC is a reusable representation of an evaluation or control principle supported by multiple sources.

We represent a CCC as:

$$
K = (R,O,P,B,E,U,V)
$$

where:

- $R$: Reusable structural or behavioral relationships;
- $O$: Associated operator semantics;
- $P$: Supported perspectives;
- $B$: Applicability boundaries;
- $E$: Supporting and counter-evidence;
- $U$: Uncertainty metadata;
- $V$: Version and validation state.

A CCC may represent a structural pattern, a behavioral principle, or a combination of both.

### 2.4 Specialized Delta

A Specialized Delta preserves source-specific information not captured by the common core.

For a source tree $T_i$, a conceptual decomposition is:

$$
T_i \approx K \oplus \Delta_i
$$

where:

- $K$: Shared core;
- $\Delta_i$: Specialized differences;
- $\oplus$: Context-dependent reconstruction or composition.

The approximation symbol is intentional.

A general lossless decomposition is not assumed.

### 2.5 CCC Forest

A CCC Forest is a collection of related reusable cores.

It may contain:

- Local CCCs;
- Domain-specific CCCs;
- Perspective-specific CCCs;
- Cross-domain CCCs;
- Experimental candidate CCCs;
- Deprecated or superseded CCCs.

The relationships need not form a strict tree.

Some cores may overlap or share substructures.

They need not always execute in a rigid linear sequence.

## 4. Stage 1 — Primitive Scoring Tree Modeling

### 4.1 Objective

Convert heterogeneous evaluation mechanisms into explicit structural representations suitable for comparison and learning.

Candidate sources include:

- Weighted scoring systems;
- Rule-based evaluators;
- ANN scoring models;
- Multi-objective decision structures;
- Branching policies;
- Relay control mechanisms;
- RSI evaluation pipelines.

### 4.2 Structural Extraction

For each source evaluator, identify:

1. Input objects;
2. Evaluation objectives;
3. Scoring operators;
4. Dependency relationships;
5. Aggregation mechanisms;
6. Constraints;
7. Evidence sources;
8. Decision outputs;
9. Applicability conditions.

### 4.3 Normalized Structural Representation

A normalized representation may use a contract-oriented node schema.

```text
Scoring Node
├── Node Identity
├── Operator Type
├── Input Contract
├── Output Contract
├── Evaluation Semantics
├── Perspective
├── Constraints
├── Evidence References
├── Uncertainty
└── Version
```

Normalization does not imply that every evaluator must be converted into the same mathematical form.

Different score types and control semantics should remain distinguishable.

### 4.4 Structural Fingerprints

For efficient localization, a Scoring Tree may be assigned a structural fingerprint.

Potential fingerprint features include:

- Operator distribution;
- Dependency topology;
- Constraint patterns;
- Evaluation objectives;
- Semantic embeddings;
- Behavioral signatures.

These fingerprints support approximate candidate retrieval.

They do not establish semantic equivalence by themselves.

### 4.5 Modeling Output

The output of Stage 1 is a collection of contract-described Scoring Trees.

Each tree should preserve sufficient provenance to trace its relationship to the original evaluator.

---

## 5. Stage 2 — Sampled Scoring Forest Construction

### 5.1 Objective

Organize scoring structures into a forest suitable for progressive commonality discovery.

### 5.2 Sampling Sources

Scoring Trees may originate from:

- Different tasks;
- Different domains;
- Different intelligence engines;
- Different perspectives;
- Different system versions;
- Successful and unsuccessful experiments.

Importantly, unsuccessful experiments may provide valuable counter-evidence.

### 5.3 Family Construction

A Scoring Forest may be partitioned into candidate families according to:

- Functional objective;
- Operator composition;
- Domain;
- Perspective;
- Structural similarity;
- Behavioral similarity;
- Applicable constraints.

Family assignments should remain revisable.

### 5.4 Diversity Preservation

A forest constructed only from highly similar successful examples may produce misleadingly narrow CCCs.

Sampling should therefore preserve diversity across:

- Domains;
- Task difficulty;
- Risk regimes;
- Resource conditions;
- Evaluation outcomes;
- Failure modes.

### 5.5 Sampling Bias

Repeated samples from the same model, training distribution, or control strategy may be strongly correlated.

The system should record provenance and avoid treating correlated observations as independent evidence.

### 5.6 Forest Output

The result is a set of candidate Scoring Tree families with explicit metadata.

These families provide the initial search space for Unaligned AND Folding.

## 6. Stage 3 — Intra-Family Unaligned AND Folding

### 6.1 Objective

Discover reusable evaluation principles among Scoring Trees that appear functionally related but may differ structurally.

### 6.2 Semantic Localization

Before attempting expensive structural correspondence search, the system identifies candidate substructures with potentially related roles.

Localization may use:

- Operator semantics;
- Input-output contracts;
- Structural fingerprints;
- Behavioral signatures;
- Perspective metadata;
- Evidence relationships.

### 6.3 Functional Correspondence

Candidate relationships may include:

- One-to-one correspondence;
- One-to-many correspondence;
- Many-to-one correspondence;
- Conditional correspondence;
- Partial correspondence.

For example, an ANN risk score may correspond functionally to several explicit risk-checking branches.

### 6.4 Candidate Core Extraction

A candidate CCC may encode recurring principles such as:

- Constraint-first filtering;
- Confidence-based escalation;
- Cost-aware branch selection;
- Evidence-based promotion;
- Risk-sensitive stopping;
- Diversity-preserving candidate retention.

### 6.5 Structural and Behavioral CCC

Two extraction modes should be distinguished.

**Structural CCC Extraction**

Identifies common operator roles, dependency relationships, and control structures.

**Behavioral CCC Extraction**

Identifies common decision behavior under specified conditions.

A useful system may maintain both representations.

### 6.6 Validation

Candidate cores must be evaluated for:

- Semantic correctness;
- Decision preservation;
- Constraint preservation;
- Robustness;
- Applicability;
- Counter-evidence sensitivity.

### 6.7 Output

Stage 3 produces candidate local CCCs with:

- Supporting sources;
- Specialized Delta;
- Validation evidence;
- Applicability boundaries;
- Uncertainty metadata.

## 7. Stage 4 — Recursive CCC Distillation

### 7.1 Objective

Improve the organization and reuse of candidate CCCs through repeated comparison with additional scoring structures.

### 7.2 Recursive Update

Let $K_t$ represent a candidate CCC at iteration $t$.

A new source tree $T_{t+1}$ provides additional evidence.

We propose:

$$
K_{t+1} =
operatorname{Update}(K_t,T_{t+1},E_{t+1})
$$

where $E_{t+1}$ includes supporting and contradictory evidence.

### 7.3 Update Outcomes

The update operation may produce:

1. **Reinforcement:** Additional evidence supports the existing core.
2. **Refinement:** The core becomes more precise.
3. **Specialization:** The core is restricted to a narrower applicability boundary.
4. **Expansion:** A broader core becomes supportable.
5. **Split:** The core is divided into distinct specialized cores.
6. **Rejection:** The proposed core is invalidated.
7. **No Change:** Evidence is insufficient to justify modification.

### 7.4 Progressive Folding

A candidate progression is:

```text
Scoring Trees
    |
    +--> Local CCC-A
    |
    +--> Local CCC-B
    |
    +--> Local CCC-C
              |
              v
      Cross-CCC Comparison
              |
              v
       Candidate Shared Core
              |
              v
       Independent Validation
              |
              v
       Promote / Split / Reject
```

### 7.5 Why Recursive Distillation May Help

Repeated structural comparison may reveal recurring evaluation principles that are difficult to identify from individual samples.

Potential benefits include:

- Reduced duplication;
- Improved reuse;
- More explicit evaluation semantics;
- Better transfer;
- More efficient future localization.

These benefits remain empirical hypotheses.

### 7.6 Non-Monotonic Learning

The CCC quality trajectory need not improve monotonically.

New evidence may reveal that an earlier abstraction was overgeneralized.

A robust system must support non-monotonic revision.

### 7.7 Versioned CCC Evolution

Every promoted CCC should retain:

- Version identity;
- Source lineage;
- Supporting evidence;
- Counter-evidence;
- Validation results;
- Applicability boundaries;
- Change history.

This enables controlled evolution and rollback.

## 8. Stage 5 — Cross-Family Universal CCC Extraction

### 8.1 Objective

Investigate whether reusable evaluation principles exist across different Scoring Tree families.

For example, technical, economic, and strategic evaluators may all implement forms of:

- Resource-aware selection;
- Risk-sensitive stopping;
- Evidence-dependent escalation;
- Constraint preservation.

### 8.2 Cross-Family Folding

Let:

$$
\mathcal{K} =
\{K_1,K_2,\ldots,K_m\}
$$

represent candidate CCCs extracted from different families.

A cross-family Folding operation may produce:

$$
K_U =
F_{\mathrm{cross}}(\mathcal{K},P,E)
$$

where $K_U$ is a candidate broader CCC.

### 8.3 Universal Is a Research Objective

The term *Universal CCC* does not imply that one core must apply to every domain.

A more realistic result may be:

- A collection of broadly reusable control principles;
- Multiple overlapping CCC families;
- Explicit domain-specific exceptions;
- Perspective-dependent compositions.

### 8.4 Commonality Versus Overgeneralization

A broad CCC should not be preferred merely because it applies to more source structures.

Its value depends on:

- Correctness;
- Decision preservation;
- Transferability;
- Interpretability;
- Computational cost;
- Applicability clarity.

### 8.5 Cross-Family Counter-Evidence

Cross-family transfer should be tested against held-out tasks and domains.

A principle that works in software engineering may not transfer directly to safety-critical control or financial decision-making.

### 8.6 Output

Stage 5 produces candidate cross-family CCC structures with explicit applicability and validation metadata.

## 9. Stage 6 — Evidence-Driven Structural Growth

### 9.1 Objective

Transform CCC extraction from a one-time compression procedure into an ongoing structural learning process.

### 9.2 Evidence Sources

New evidence may originate from:

- AI Relay execution;
- RSI experiments;
- Human evaluation;
- External tests;
- New scoring models;
- Domain-specific failures;
- Counterexamples;
- Deployment feedback.

### 9.3 Growth Operations

The CCC system may support:

**G-ADD**

Introduce a new scoring principle.

**G-REFINE**

Improve an existing principle.

**G-SPECIALIZE**

Restrict applicability.

**G-SPLIT**

Divide an overgeneralized core.

**G-MERGE**

Combine compatible cores.

**G-RETIRE**

Remove a core from active use.

**G-ROLLBACK**

Restore a previously validated version.

### 9.4 Counter-Evidence as a Growth Trigger

Counter-evidence should not be treated merely as a negative score.

It may indicate a missing structural distinction.

For example:

A CCC that assumes a low-cost evaluator is sufficient for routine tasks may fail when rare high-impact risks are present.

The failure may motivate a new conditional branch:

```text
Routine Task
    |
    v
Cheap Scoring
    |
    v
Rare-Risk Trigger?
    |
    +-- No --> Continue
    |
    +-- Yes --> Rich Risk Evaluation
```

This is structural growth rather than simple parameter adjustment.

### 9.5 Open-Ended Growth

A CCC Forest may accept:

- New operator types;
- New perspectives;
- New scoring structures;
- New validation mechanisms;
- New specialized intelligence engines.

The architecture should remain extensible rather than assuming a closed set of evaluation concepts.

## 10. CCC Lifecycle and Promotion Governance

### 10.1 Lifecycle States

We propose the following lifecycle:

```text
Candidate
    |
    v
Under Validation
    |
    +--> Rejected
    |
    v
Validated
    |
    v
Promoted
    |
    +--> Specialized
    |
    +--> Superseded
    |
    +--> Rolled Back
    |
    v
Retired
```

### 10.2 Candidate

A newly extracted CCC has not yet demonstrated adequate reliability.

### 10.3 Under Validation

The candidate undergoes:

- Structural verification;
- Behavioral testing;
- Constraint checking;
- Counter-evidence search;
- Transfer evaluation.

### 10.4 Validated

A candidate is validated only within specified applicability conditions.

Validation is not a universal guarantee.

### 10.5 Promoted

A promoted CCC becomes available for controlled reuse.

Promotion should require explicit acceptance criteria.

### 10.6 Specialized

A CCC may be restricted to a narrower domain or perspective after new evidence reveals exceptions.

### 10.7 Superseded

A newer validated CCC may replace an earlier version.

### 10.8 Rolled Back

A promoted CCC may be reverted if subsequent evidence reveals unacceptable behavior.

### 10.9 Retired

A CCC may be removed from active selection while preserving its historical record.

### 10.10 Governance Principle

**A CCC should not authorize its own promotion solely through its internal scoring mechanism.**

Independent evaluation and policy enforcement are required.

## 11. Core, Delta, and Applicability Boundaries

### 11.1 The Core-Delta Representation

A useful folded structure may be represented as:

$$
T_i \approx K \oplus \Delta_i
$$

where:

- $K$: Shared core;
- $\Delta_i$: Specialized difference.

### 11.2 Delta Is Not Noise

Specialized Delta may contain:

- Domain-specific evaluation criteria;
- Rare exception handling;
- Perspective-dependent constraints;
- Alternative aggregation logic;
- New structural innovations.

Removing Delta indiscriminately may degrade decision quality.

### 11.3 Applicability Boundary

Every CCC should specify the conditions under which it has been tested.

Possible dimensions include:

- Domain;
- Task type;
- Risk regime;
- Input distribution;
- Resource budget;
- Perspective;
- Governance context.

### 11.4 Multiple Core Membership

A Scoring Tree may participate in several CCC families.

For example, the same evaluator may share:

- A risk-control core with safety systems;
- A cost-control core with resource schedulers;
- A branch-selection core with AI Relay systems.

This supports overlapping CCC Forests.

### 11.5 Localization Before Reuse

A CCC should be selected through applicability-aware localization.

Reusing a core outside its validated conditions should trigger additional evaluation or escalation.

## 12. Decision Preservation and Distillation Quality

### 12.1 Preservation Objective

Recursive distillation should preserve decision-relevant information.

A basic criterion is decision agreement:

$$
Q_D =
\Pr_{(x,P)\sim\mathcal{D}}
[D(K,x,P)=D(T,x,P)]
$$

where:

- $K$: Candidate CCC-based evaluator;
- $T$: Reference evaluator;
- $\mathcal{D}$: Validation distribution.

### 12.2 Utility Preservation

Decision agreement alone is insufficient.

A candidate CCC should also be evaluated through downstream utility.

$$
Q_U =
\mathbb{E}[U(D(K,x,P))] - \mathbb{E}[U(D(T,x,P))]
$$

### 12.3 Constraint Preservation

Mandatory constraints must be checked independently.

A CCC should not be promoted if it introduces unacceptable policy or safety violations.

### 12.4 Robustness

Validation should include:

- Perturbed inputs;
- Distribution shifts;
- Adversarial examples;
- Rare-event conditions;
- Conflicting evidence;
- Unseen domains.

### 12.5 Compression and Complexity

Structural compression may be measured through:

- Node reduction;
- Edge reduction;
- Shared operator reuse;
- Memory footprint;
- Evaluation latency.

However, compression should not dominate quality or safety requirements.

### 12.6 Candidate Quality Objective

A possible research objective is:

$$
J(K) =
\alpha Q_D +
\beta Q_U +
\gamma Q_R +
\delta Q_T - \lambda C_K
$$

where:

- $Q_D$: Decision preservation;
- $Q_U$: Utility preservation;
- $Q_R$: Robustness;
- $Q_T$: Transferability;
- $C_K$: Complexity or cost.

The weights are perspective-dependent.

Hard constraints should remain separate from compensatory objectives.

## 13. Recursive Distillation Algorithm Skeleton

The following pseudocode summarizes the proposed process.

```text
Algorithm: Recursive-CCC-Distillation

Input:
    Scoring Tree collection T
    Perspective set P
    Evidence repository E
    Contract rules C
    Preservation requirements R

Output:
    Versioned CCC Forest K
    Specialized Delta repository D
    Validation and audit records A

1. Normalize source Scoring Trees.

2. Record provenance, contracts, and perspectives.

3. Construct candidate Scoring Tree families.

4. For each family:
       Localize candidate common substructures.
       Generate functional correspondences.
       Perform Unaligned AND Folding.
       Extract candidate Structural CCCs.
       Extract candidate Behavioral CCCs.
       Preserve unmatched Specialized Delta.

5. Validate candidate CCCs:
       Test decision preservation.
       Test utility retention.
       Test constraints.
       Test robustness.
       Search for counter-evidence.

6. Promote validated local CCCs.

7. Compare promoted CCCs across families.

8. Extract candidate cross-family CCCs.

9. Validate cross-family transfer.

10. Promote, specialize, split, or reject candidates.

11. For each new evidence batch:
        Identify affected CCCs.
        Re-evaluate applicability.
        Refine or split cores as necessary.
        Preserve previous validated versions.
        Roll back invalid promotions.

12. Update CCC Forest indexes and lineage.

13. Return versioned CCC Forest,
    Specialized Delta, and audit records.
```

This algorithm is a conceptual research skeleton.

Implementation details such as correspondence search, data structures, complexity bounds, and promotion thresholds remain open.

## 14. Incremental and Cost-Aware Distillation

### 14.1 Why Incremental Learning Matters

Reprocessing an entire Scoring Forest whenever new evidence appears may be computationally expensive.

An incremental approach may update only affected CCC families.

### 14.2 Localized Update

A new scoring structure may first be matched against existing CCC indexes.

Possible outcomes include:

1. Reuse an existing CCC;
2. Extend a compatible CCC;
3. Create a specialized Delta;
4. Propose a new CCC;
5. Trigger revalidation of an existing core.

### 14.3 Two-Phase Localization

A practical system may use:

**Phase 1 — Cheap Candidate Retrieval**

Structural fingerprints, embeddings, and contract filtering.

**Phase 2 — Rich Validation**

Detailed correspondence analysis, behavioral testing, and counter-evidence evaluation.

### 14.4 Distillation Cost

Let:

$$
C_{\mathrm{total}} =
C_{\mathrm{extract}} +
C_{\mathrm{validate}} +
C_{\mathrm{maintain}} +
C_{\mathrm{reuse}}
$$

represent total lifecycle cost.

The economic value of a CCC depends on whether future reuse benefits justify extraction and maintenance expenses.

### 14.5 Perspective-Aware Investment

A Control Plane may allocate more distillation resources to:

- Frequently reused evaluation structures;
- High-value decision domains;
- Expensive recurring evaluations;
- High-risk control mechanisms;
- Promising cross-domain transfer opportunities.

Long-tail cases may require preservation without expensive global optimization.

## 15. ANN Distillation and Structural CCC Distillation

### 15.1 Two Complementary Forms of Distillation

UCPSF distinguishes:

**Structural CCC Distillation**

Extracts explicit reusable evaluation relationships.

**ANN Distillation**

Trains a learned model to approximate an evaluation process.

### 15.2 Structural Distillation

Advantages may include:

- Explicit dependencies;
- Interpretability;
- Reusable control structure;
- Clear applicability metadata.

Limitations may include:

- Alignment complexity;
- Structural modeling overhead;
- Difficulty representing some distributed functions.

### 15.3 ANN Distillation

Advantages may include:

- Efficient repeated inference;
- Flexible function approximation;
- Compact learned representations.

Limitations may include:

- Calibration problems;
- Distribution shift;
- Reduced interpretability;
- Hidden evaluation bias.

### 15.4 Hybrid Distillation

A candidate architecture combines both:

```text
Rich Scoring Forest
        |
        v
Structural CCC Extraction
        |
        +------------------+
        |                  |
        v                  v
Explicit CCC          ANN Distillation
        |                  |
        +------------------+
                 |
                 v
        Hybrid Scoring Node
                 |
                 v
       Validation / Escalation
```

The explicit CCC may provide structural control and applicability constraints.

The ANN may provide efficient approximation within validated regions.

### 15.5 Escalation

When a learned scorer encounters uncertainty or novelty, the Control Plane may invoke the richer structural evaluator.

This supports adaptive evaluation cost.

## 17. Application to Structural RSI

### 17.1 RSI Requires Rich Evaluation

Structural RSI modifies the organization of intelligence systems.

Candidate changes may affect:

- Localization;
- Per-node computation;
- Scoring structures;
- Control policies;
- Structural representations.

Such changes require evaluation across multiple dimensions.

### 17.2 CCC as an RSI Resource

A validated scoring CCC may provide reusable evaluation logic for future RSI experiments.

This may reduce repeated evaluator construction.

### 17.3 Recursive Improvement of Evaluators

The evaluation system itself may become an RSI target.

For example:

1. An RSI experiment reveals a missing risk criterion.
2. Counter-evidence triggers a scoring-structure revision.
3. A new CCC candidate is extracted.
4. Independent tests evaluate the revised core.
5. The validated version is promoted.

### 17.4 The Evaluator Drift Problem

A system that changes both its behavior and its evaluation criteria may appear to improve without achieving genuine external progress.

Accordingly, evaluator modifications require independent reference tests and governance controls.

### 17.5 Controlled RSI Loop

          |

Promote / Reject / Revise
          |

New Evidence

## 18. Relationship to Universal Control Planes

### 18.1 CCC Forest as a Control Resource

A Universal Control Plane may maintain a registry of validated CCCs.

Each core can be indexed by:

- Domain;
- Perspective;
- Task type;
- Operator family;
- Applicability boundary;
- Validation status;
- Resource cost.

### 18.2 Control Plane Responsibilities

The Control Plane may:

1. Localize applicable CCCs;
2. Select evaluation structures;
3. Allocate scoring resources;
4. Invoke ANN or explicit evaluators;
5. Detect conflicts;
6. Trigger rich validation;
7. Manage CCC lifecycle transitions;
8. Enforce policy constraints.

### 18.3 CCC Selection Is Itself a Scoring Problem

Selecting an appropriate CCC requires evaluation of:

- Applicability;
- Reliability;
- Cost;
- Perspective alignment;
- Risk.

This creates a meta-scoring problem.

UCPSF-005 develops Perspective-Aware Optimization and Meta-Scoring Intelligence in greater detail.

### 18.4 Separation of Scoring and Authorization

A CCC may recommend a decision.

It should not automatically authorize external action.

Authorization remains subject to independent policy constraints.

## 19. Validation Framework

### 19.1 Research Hypotheses

**H1 — Reusable Commonality**

Heterogeneous Scoring Trees contain reusable evaluation principles that can be extracted through structural and behavioral correspondence.

**H2 — Progressive Improvement**

Evidence-driven recursive distillation can improve CCC validity or reuse under specified conditions.

**H3 — Transferability**

Some CCCs can transfer across related domains while preserving decision quality.

**H4 — Cost Efficiency**

Incremental CCC reuse can reduce repeated evaluation or engineering costs when reuse benefits exceed extraction and maintenance costs.

**H5 — Counter-Evidence Robustness**

Explicit counter-evidence mechanisms can reduce overgeneralization and evaluator drift.

### 19.2 Baselines

Candidate baselines include:

- Independent scoring systems;
- Exact structural matching;
- Flat ensemble scoring;
- Static manually designed evaluation trees;
- ANN distillation without structural CCC;
- Non-incremental Folding;
- Recursive Folding without counter-evidence validation.

### 19.3 Experimental Campaigns

**Campaign A — Local CCC Extraction**

Test commonality extraction among related Scoring Trees.

**Campaign B — Recursive Distillation**

Measure changes in CCC validity and reuse as additional evidence is introduced.

**Campaign C — Cross-Domain Transfer**

Evaluate CCC performance on held-out domains.

**Campaign D — Counter-Evidence**

Inject challenging cases and measure whether the system specializes, splits, or rejects invalid cores.

**Campaign E — Cost-Aware Incremental Learning**

Compare full recomputation with localized CCC updates.

**Campaign F — Hybrid Structural and ANN Distillation**

Compare explicit CCC evaluation, learned approximation, and hybrid escalation.

## 20. Risks and Limitations

### 20.1 False Commonality

Different scoring structures may appear similar without sharing transferable principles.

### 20.2 Correlated Samples

Large sample counts may provide little additional evidence if the samples share the same underlying bias.

### 20.3 Overgeneralization

Cross-family Folding may produce cores that are too broad to preserve important distinctions.

### 20.4 Long-Tail Loss

Rare but critical evaluation branches may be removed during compression.

### 20.5 Recursive Drift

Repeated updates may weaken the relationship between scoring structures and external objectives.

### 20.6 Complexity Growth

A CCC Forest may become difficult to manage if overlapping cores proliferate without effective indexing and lifecycle controls.

### 20.7 Governance Failures

Improper promotion or reuse may cross policy boundaries.

### 20.8 Validation Cost

Independent testing and counter-evidence search may be computationally expensive.

These risks motivate localized evaluation, explicit contracts, versioned evidence, and controlled lifecycle management.

## 22. Conclusion

This paper proposes a six-stage framework for Common Concept Core Extraction and Recursive Distillation across heterogeneous Scoring Trees and Scoring Forests.

The framework begins with explicit scoring-structure modeling and proceeds through sampled forest construction, intra-family Unaligned AND Folding, recursive CCC distillation, cross-family commonality extraction, and evidence-driven structural growth.

Its central principle is:

> **A Common Concept Core should be treated as a revisable, evidence-supported structural hypothesis rather than a permanently established universal truth.**

The proposed architecture preserves:

1. Reusable common evaluation principles;
2. Specialized Delta;
3. Perspective and applicability boundaries;
4. Supporting and counter-evidence;
5. Validation status and version history;
6. Reversible structural growth.

The ultimate objective is not maximal compression into one universal scoring tree.

It is the progressive development of a reusable and governable **CCC Scoring Forest**.

Such a forest may provide an important foundation for Generalized Scoring Intelligence, Universal Control Planes, AI Relay, Hybrid Structural Intelligence, and Structural Recursive Self-Improvement.

The next research step is to determine how these evolving scoring structures should be selected and evaluated under different perspectives, computational budgets, time horizons, and risk regimes.

That problem is addressed in UCPSF-005.
